# Galápagos Cruises: How to Choose, and What Each Ship Type Actually Gets You

There is no single best Galápagos cruise — only the best fit for you. The choice comes down to four things: vessel tier, trip length, island region, and season. This guide gives you the real trade-offs, not a sales pitch. We're an independent guide maintained by {{BRAND}}, and we'll give you the same answer whether or not you book through us.

## What a Galápagos cruise costs, plainly

Cruises run roughly **$2,500 to $20,000+ per person** for **4 to 15 days** [source: MASTER_FACTS; galapagos_ships.xlsx]. Mainland flights to the islands (Quito or Guayaquil → Baltra or San Cristóbal) add about **$400–$500** and take ~3 hours — fly in a day early [source: MASTER_FACTS]. Two costs are extra on every ship, at every price: the **$200 national park entrance fee** and the **~$20 INGALA transit card** [source: MASTER_FACTS]. Anyone who folds those into a headline price is rounding; we'd rather you see them.

## The four tiers — what changes, and what doesn't

The wildlife is the same across tiers — every vessel visits the same National Park-authorized sites on a managed schedule. What changes is group size, comfort, and guide ratio.

- **Budget / tourist class** — roughly $2,500–$4,500 pp. Smaller cabins, fewer amenities, core wildlife access. The animals are indifferent to what you paid for your berth.
- **First-class expedition** — the frequently-misunderstood middle. Strong naturalist programs and intimate group sizes at a fraction of ultra-premium cost [source: galapagos_ships.xlsx].
- **Luxury small ships** — premium comfort and multiple naturalist guides; ranges from 20-guest yachts to 100-guest expedition ships at the legal maximum.
- **Ultra-premium yachts (8–16 guests)** — the smallest groups and best guide ratios; the whole party goes ashore together, no staggered rotations.

> **Worried about choosing wrong?** That's the most common feeling at this stage. Tell us your dates, budget and who's traveling, and a specialist will match you to the right tier — across operators, not one boat.

## "Will it be crowded?"

Every ship lands at the same regulated sites; the difference you feel is group size. A 16-guest yacht and a 100-guest ship are very different ashore — smaller vessels land as one group, larger ships rotate parties by Zodiac [source: galapagos_source_of_truth.xlsx, Visitor Site Access]. Neither is wrong; it's a preference.

## How long, and which islands

Plan **at least 4–5 days** for the central islands and **7–8 days** to reach the wildlife-rich outer and western islands [source: MASTER_FACTS]. Several outer, uninhabited sites — Fernandina, Genovesa, parts of Isabela, Española — are reachable **only by liveaboard cruise**; day-trips from the inhabited islands reach about a third of the sites [source: galapagos_source_of_truth.xlsx]. If a specific island or species is on your list, the itinerary matters more than the ship.

## When to go

The Galápagos is a year-round destination with two seasons: the **warm, wetter season (Dec–May)** brings calmer seas and active courtship displays, and the **cool, dry "garúa" season (Jun–Nov)** brings nutrient-rich water, the best snorkelling, and active seabirds [source: MASTER_FACTS]. There's no wrong month — only different wildlife. See our full best-time guide for the month-by-month calendar.

## Traveling solo, or with family?

- **Solo:** the solo supplement is real but not universal — some operators waive or reduce it on selected departures. Give us your dates and we'll find those.
- **Families:** minimum ages vary by vessel, and several ships run family departures with triple or connecting cabins. We'll tell you which suit your kids' ages.
- **Mobility:** most days involve wet or dry panga landings and some walking on uneven volcanic ground. Several ships suit active travelers with limited mobility well — tell us your needs and we'll match the right one.
