Dear Whalebig,
you ask a fundamental question. A question everyone should ask before deciding on a trip to Galapagos.
Going there is expensive in time, money and effort. Being sure you are exposed to the best of what the Galapagos has to offer after you have arrived (as you should) is expensive as well. And we’re becoming more conscious of the environmental impact that comes with jetting around the world.
We owe it to ourselves, to Galapagos and to the planet to be sure about our motivations, and to carefully judge whether the cost is worth the reward.
You can see a lot of animals in a zoo – so as Tina notes, it’s not about checking an animal off your list. If that’s the only reason you are going – maybe it’s best to give it a pass.
You have to consider Galapagos as the most beautiful cathedral in the world (think of Chartres in France, for instance…). If you’re very interested in gothic architecture, sure, there are hundreds of churches and many cathedrals you can visit (and probably a few “faux gothic” buildings in Jersey City) - but until you’ve visited Chartres, you haven’t reached the top.
Galapagos is the Chartres of wildlife/evolutionary biology destinations. A truly complete visit should consist just as much a mind trip as a physical one. Being able to get into Darwin’s head while your there, to see how what he saw helped sparked radical shift in how humans understand their place in the word can be a humbling experience – just as it’s a humbling experience for an enthusiast of Gothic architecture to enter into the Chartres cathedral – after having anticipated that moment for many years.
You should get goosebumps when you first set foot in Galapagos.
Of course, Galapagos is a whole lot of fun. Snorkeling with playful sealions is one of the most memorable wildlife encounters people remark upon. Hiking over fresh lava fields, and coming across marine iguanas, giant tortoises, land iguanas, lava lizards, snakes… Did you know that Galapagos is the only place left in the world where reptiles still dominate the land?
Underwater Galapagos is a big part of the thrill, watching penguins dart about, a school of rays glide by in such a zen manner, sharks and sea turtles (so many that you feel you’re in a sea turtle soup). You might chance upon a pod of 1,000 dolphins – but you’ll certainly come across some bow riding in front of your ship. These encounters, and more like them, should bring out a sense of wonder that perhaps you haven’t felt since you were a child.
So, there are my two cents’ worth. I do think it’s a good investment of your time to prepare for this trip – to get some preliminary understanding on how this archipelago contributed to Darwin’s thinking. It will make your visit that much more rewarding.

These establishments were doing their best to meet regulatory standards (exits marked, smoke detectors, sound electrical systems, fire extinguishers etc.... that sort of thing), it was costing them money, but they saw that the "under-the-radar" folks were not doing anything, not investing and able to run their unsafe establishments at lower prices..... so in an effort to support those establishments that were following the rules, the government imposed this "you must book in a registered place and show proof" requirement.
But it did not work. To get around it, folks would book an AirBnB, print off their proof of booking, then they would immediately cancel it... there were too many leaks in the system. So the authorities dropped that requirement. Result: There is still a bit of a "wild west" situation going on with accommodations in Galapagos, with non-registered folks undermining the establishment of a well-regulated, higher standards approach.

Dear BF,
there are about 65 ships plying Galpagos waters. About 50 of these carry 20 or fewer passengers. Only about 4 - 5 carry 100 (or close to it) passengers. They are all on the high end in terms of $$ (starting at about US$1,000 / person / day on board). They are the big international (ta && ta.queueForLoad ? ta.queueForLoad : function(f, g){document.addEventListener('DOMContentLoaded', f);})(function(){ta.trackEventOnPage('postLinkInline', 'impression', 'postLinks-116687094', '');}, 'log_autolink_impression');cruise ship companies: Celebrity, Silversea, Hurtigurten, National Geographic/Lindblad.
I have been sending people on Galapagos cruises since 1999 - but only about 1% of my guests get on a larger ship. We survey returning guests about their experience. One question is "how much did seasickness affect your enjoyment of the trip" and 91.8% respond "not at all (60.4%)" or "just a little bit (30.4%)". Only 2.5% said "quite a bit" and 0% said "I wanted to get off the ship".
I think concern over seasickness in Galapagos is misplaced. After a day on a ship, almost everyone gets their sea legs. And there are patches etc... to guard against it. People who worry about getting seasick often (not always of course) base that on a short boat ride they took at another time (i.e. 2 hours....). A multi-day boat ride is a very different story.
I generally discourage large ships, because they just don't provide the same serendipitous opportunities to make the most out of your time among the islands, with so many people on board, it's a lot harder to take advantage of things that might just appear out at sea.
For example, last week, one ship we use a lot (14 passenger Samba) saw some strange activity off a little ways from their usual course and decided to go check things out (large ships do not stray off course). They came upon a group of about 40 whale sharks (number is according to the naturalist guide), in a bit of a feeding frenzy at the surface of the sea - a very exceptional display. The same group earlier that day also came upon a group of pilot whales and was able to snorkel with them. These are the kinds of things a 100 passenger ship simply cannot engage in.
Another thing to consider is time of year. Seas are at their calmest between January and April/May.
I hope this helps.

Dear Shani,
the first thing to do when considering a trip to Galapagos is determining how you'll visit the islands. There are two ways:
1) land based, where you stay in town and participate in day trips (generally cheaper)
2) ship based, where you stay on a small (12-20, but a few with 100 passengers) and explore the archipelago (exposes you to more "Galapagos" in terms of wildlife, landscapes)
Have you looked at the pros and cons of both? I recommend doing so, and deciding which model is best for you, before moving on to step two, which is working out the details.
If you've decided on the land-based, I would recommend identifying a reputable local travel company (there are a numbrer of good ones based in Galapagos) who could work with you to organize your daily plan, and help with bookings. It would make life a lot easier as the range of choices / budgets varies tremendously. Of course, local agencies are not regulated as strictly as those in the UK (who may just sell you something "off the rack", having to real on-the-ground expertise), which may justifiably play a part in your choice of agency.
