Dear DJ,
you are in Galapagos to observe nature - and that doesn't happen while on grueling hikes. A typical outing while on a (ta && ta.queueForLoad ? ta.queueForLoad : function(f, g){document.addEventListener('DOMContentLoaded', f);})(function(){ta.trackEventOnPage('postLinkInline', 'impression', 'postLinks-79617191', '');}, 'log_autolink_impression');cruise can have you up quite early (e.g. 6AM? depending on the ship, the visitor site, the naturalist guide). If you consider that physically grueling (it can be, I grant you that!), then that could be an issue for you. You will embark on a small zodiac and be taken to an isolated landing spot on an island. Getting in and out of the zodiac will demand a bit of dexterity, but thousands of people do it every year, and you'll be helped by your guide and the ship's crew. Once on land, you'll be taken for a quite leisurely walk for about 1-2 hours, typically covering no more than 1, at the most 2 miles (1.5 - 3km). Doing the maths, you'll realize that this is a very slow walk- punctuated with many stops to observe nature. There may be a bit of uphill, but nothing Everest-like. Perhaps the hardest part is dealing with the sun - which can be strong (bring a great hat, or even an umbrella, which can double as a walking stick). Expect 2 such outings per day.
You will be given the opportunity to snorkel quite often and for varying periods of time (again, depending on the ship etc...). The underwater world in Galapagos is very much (more?) part of the overall experience and shouldn't be missed. A good ship will be able to accommodate all levels of snorkelers, and yes, life jackets can be used (or other flotation devices - life jackets can be encumbering and ruin the experience I find).
You will always have the option to just stay on board for some quiet time if you like.

Cruise ship numbers are strictly limited in Galapagos, whereas land based infrastructure such as hotels and restaurants are very poorly regulated. For the ships, there hardly has been a capacity increase in the past 20 years. This is wonderful for conserving the very thing that people go to see in Galapagos in the first place (e.g. the wonderful diversity of unique plants and animals), and is a testament to restraint on the part of government authorities. For land based tourism, there has been an exponential growth over the past 15 years, raising concerns over the impact on the islands on the part of conservation organizations, the United Nations (this is a World Heritage site) and even ironically the government of Ecuador. See the just out latest edition of Scientific American for an article on that very issue.
The combination of i) very remote operating environment for small ship cruises and ii) limited supply of ships means that prices end up being quite a lot higher than your typical berth on a 4,000 passenger Caribbean cruise liner. Galapagos is more akin to Antarctica in terms of prices.

Water temperatures drop from a comfortably warm, no wetsuit necessary level in March/April, to fresher in May, to maybe at least a light wetsuit would be nice in June - for those not used to cooler waters (I'm from Canada, I can handle those June waters without a wetsuit). A very strong El Niño will result in warm waters throughout the May - December period, almost too warm for pleasure frankly.
The effects on ecosystems is massive - marine ecosystems suffer - nutrient rich waters become nutrient poor, and all marine life, or species dependent on marine life, suffer a great deal. Conversely, terrestrial ecosystems witness a great explosion of life - birds nest 2-3 times a season instead of only 1, the islands are green - there are even rivers - something unheard of in the Galapagos. Big downpours are more common - but you still have plenty of sun. It's warm, it's very humid.
Visiting Galapagos during an El Niño is a very rare treat. The graphic manifestation of ecosystem disruption by sudden climate change is in your face. I first visited Galapagos on the tail end of the last big El Niño, in July 1998.

Dear Lari,
it seems that the big mass of El Niño's very warm waters, up until now lying a few hundred km north of the archipelago, has just started drifting south. December - January is the transition period in a usual year from cooler, mistier garua season (June-December) to the hotter, wetter (e.g. occasional tropical downpours) wet season (Jan-May). It's normal for things to warm up this time of year. But based on my monitoring of comments from naturalist guide friends, the NOAA El Niño website, and FB posts from people in the islands, I get the impression that though the previous months had not been too affected by this year's big El Niño, it seems that the next few months might be different - that El Niño conditions are starting to manifest themselves in the islands now. The first big rain to hit Santa Cruz was just a few days ago, and it was so big that it washed out the main road to the airport - this doesn't happen in a regular year.
So, if going this year or next makes no difference to you, you might want to postpone. But if you go, you'll still get a trip of a lifetime. All the reports I have seen so far indicate a near normal situation in terms of wildlife spotting etc... For this El Niño, the hot waters receded as they usually do in May last year (2015), meaning the life cycles of plants and animals followed their usual course these past few months. In 1997-98, the hot waters did not go in May (1997), and stayed warm until June 1998, which severely affected marine ecosystem dependent wildlife.
My guess is that conditions in terms of wildlife viewing will not be as severely affected as they were in the 1997-98 El Niño, but you will have more tropical downpours than usual, and it maybe hotter, and more humid than usual.

Flattery will get you everywhere.
I am not an El Niño expert - but I would say no one can say for sure exactly how things will transpire at the level of detail we are interested in (e.g. within the Galapagos archipelago). It's important to note that the Galapagos archipelago represents a series of large obstacles in the middle of Pacific waters, exactly where warm currents (from North East to West), cold currents (South East to West) and deep cold currents rise (West to East) meet, and shift over the course of a year, according to regular patters. Throw in the El Niño, and more variables are introduced. So, you end up with massive, shifting eddies of cooler / warmer waters twisting and winding themselves around the islands, bringing different water temperatures (with differing characteristics such as oxygen, nutrient loads) to different places for different periods of time (e.g. hours, to days, even weeks).
Generally, waters warm up this time of year during a regular year, and during a strong El Niño year, they warm up more than usual. Ocean dependent wildlife can do well during normal warm water periods, but the warmer El Niño waters tend to me more than they can handle, and they suffer.
So, maybe things are cooling down - but that surprises me. Maybe this is giant the last gasp of the pre-warm season cool water, manifested in a large eddy?
May June should be when the El Niño is resolved and dissipates, and if the last big El Niño is anything to go by, things go back to normal. However, if the next few months come with unusually warm waters, it will take a few months beyond May - June for things to bounce back - a few years even if things are very bad, particularly for penguins and flightless cormorants, who have no place to hide.

Hotel-Based Tours vs. Expeditions by Cruise: Choosing Your Galapagos Adventure
Having lived in Puerto Ayora for four years, worked at the Charles Darwin Research Station, and raised my children in the islands, I constantly get asked the ultimate question: should you visit the Galapagos Islands by taking an expedition cruise, or by doing a hotel-based (land-based) island-hopping tour
? The debate is never-ending, but understanding the stark differences will help you make the best choice for your bucket-list trip
.
If you are traveling to the Galapagos, your primary motivation is likely nature observation—getting up close and personal with unique wildlife and otherworldly landscapes
. Here is a breakdown of how the two travel styles compare in delivering that experience.
Expeditions by Cruise (Ship-Based Tours) A Galapagos cruise is not a typical mega-ship vacation with casinos and endless buffets; it is a true "ocean safari" designed to maximize your exposure to the archipelago's diverse ecosystems
.
The Pros:
Efficient use of your limited time: You travel at night, meaning you wake up already anchored at your morning visitor site
.
Optimal wildlife viewing: You will be out hiking and snorkeling early in the morning and late in the afternoon when the wildlife is most active
.
Access to remote corners: Cruises take you to distant, uninhabited islands (like Fernandina and Genovesa) that day-trippers simply cannot reach, giving you a profound sense of isolation
.
Seamless travel logistics: Once you board the ship and unpack in your cabin, you never have to worry about finding restaurants, packing your bags, checking in and out of hotels, or waiting for taxis
.
Intimate encounters: Most expedition ships carry 16 to 20 passengers, ensuring a highly personalized experience with your certified naturalist guides
.
The Cons:
Higher upfront costs: Cruises typically cost between 50% to 100% more than a land-based trip of a similar comfort level, with standard prices ranging from $350 to over $1,100 per person, per day
.
Digital detox: Internet and cell service are scarce, usually only available when the ship is anchored near main towns
.
Hotel-Based Tours (Land-Based / Island Hopping) A land-based tour involves staying in towns like Puerto Ayora (Santa Cruz) or Puerto Villamil (Isabela) and taking daily speedboat trips to nearby visitor sites
.
The Pros:
Budget-friendly flexibility: You have complete control over your spending
. You can survive on as little as $60 a day at modest hostels and local eateries, or spend $800+ a day at high-end boutique hotels
.
Free time and local culture: You have the freedom to skip a day of touring to just relax at a beach, enjoy the local town culture, and try different restaurants at your own pace
.
The Cons:
Exhausting daily logistics: A significant portion of your daylight hours is consumed by "commuting"
. You will spend hours getting to and from the docks, packing and unpacking between islands, and waiting for group members
.
Bone-jarring speedboat rides: Traveling to visitor sites often requires enduring 2 to 3-hour rides on small, fast speedboats
. In my experience, these rough rides induce seasickness far more often than the slower, stable cruise ships
.
Poor timing for wildlife: National Park regulations prevent land-based day-trippers from arriving at shared visitor sites before 10:00 AM
. By this time, the equatorial sun is at its zenith, and the animals are hiding from the heat to rest
.
Restricted access and crowds: Due to the skyrocketing number of land-based tourists, the National Park has restricted day-trippers from certain iconic snorkel sites, like Kicker Rock, to manage the overwhelming pressure on areas close to inhabited islands
.
The Conservation Reality It is vital to recognize the environmental footprint of your choice. Cruise ship capacity has been strictly capped for over 20 years, remaining flat at around 70,000 to 72,000 passengers annually
. Meanwhile, land-based tourism has exploded from nearly zero in the 1990s to over 200,000 visitors a year, leading to overcrowding at local sites and increased pressure on town infrastructure
.
The Final Takeaway If your primary goal is to get the absolute most "Galapagos" out of your visit—meaning you want intimate, high-quality encounters with wildlife and want to witness the graphic manifestations of biological evolution—an expedition cruise is hands-down the superior choice
.
However, if your budget is strictly limited, or you highly value unstructured free time in town over maximizing your time in the wilderness, a land-based trip is a completely viable way to experience the Enchanted Isles
.

Discuss Snorkeling and wildlife.

Snorkeling in the Galapagos is arguably half of the "WOW" factor of the entire trip
. Unlike destinations such as the Caribbean, there are no coral reefs here
. The underwater experience is entirely focused on marine megafauna.
Wildlife Encounters You can expect intimate encounters with unique marine life. Common sightings include:
Sea lions, which often act like "underwater puppies" and frolic around you
.
Sea turtles in such abundance that it can feel like swimming in "turtle soup"
.
Galapagos penguins darting through the water like torpedoes
.
Schools of rays (such as spotted eagle rays or mobula rays) swimming in formation
.
Harmless sharks, including Galapagos sharks and hammerheads
.
Rare but possible encounters with dolphins, orcas, or whale sharks
.
While the animals generally do not fear humans, it is critical to remember they are wild. You must maintain a 2-meter distance at all times and NEVER attempt to touch or pet them, as sea lions can deliver a dangerous bite
.
Water Conditions and Gear Galapagos waters are nutrient-rich, meaning visibility is often murky and varies from 7 to 50 meters
. However, this is rarely a problem because the wildlife comes so close to you
.
Hot Season (January to April/May): Waters are warmer, typically between 70F and 77F (20C to 25C)
. A "shorty" wetsuit or simply a swimsuit is often sufficient, depending on your tolerance
.
Cool/Garua Season (June to December): Water temperatures drop significantly. A 3mm to 5mm full wetsuit, along with a neoprene beanie and booties, is highly recommended to prevent chilling
.
Sun Protection: The equatorial sun is brutal. Wearing a full-length Lycra dive skin or long-sleeved rash guard will protect your back and thighs from severe sunburn and occasional stinging organisms like thimble jellyfish
.
Skill Level You do not need to be a strong swimmer to enjoy snorkeling, as the activity simply requires floating on the surface with your face in the water
. If you are apprehensive, naturalist guides and panga (dinghy) drivers are always nearby to assist
. You can wear a snorkeling vest for extra buoyancy, but avoid traditional life jackets, which are designed to keep your head out of the water and make snorkeling difficult
.
If you choose to sit out the snorkeling excursions, you can often opt for a panga ride along the shoreline to spot wildlife, and a few larger ships even offer glass-bottom boats
.
