Almost all of them carry 20 or fewer passengers. About 4 carry the maximum allowable 100 passengers and a handful carry between 28 and 90 passengers. The larger ships are typically part of global cruise lines (Celebrity, Silversea etc...).
Some travel companies will have relationships with only a small number of ships. Some companies charter ships on an annual basis and work exclusively with those (see G-Adventures for example). Your "main street" travel company may only know the ships they find on their global cruise database - meaning the Celebrity, Silversea, Lindblad brands. A good Galapagos specialist travel company will be familiar with most ships in the islands while not being beholden to any particular ship. It will focus first on your interests / needs first, and based on those, suggest ships that they feel best suit you.

You can understand that the ship owner doesn't want travel companies to undercut their prices. Generally, poor quality travel companies will do that, to make the sale. The Angy, an interisland ferry, sank yesterday afternoon shortly before arriving into Puerto Ayora (Santa Cruz island), coming in from Villamil (Isabela Island). Two people are reported to have perished, seven still missing as of last night, some rescued.
Interisland ferries are infamous for their very rough ride. They typically have 2 or 3 outboard motors of 250hp each and are designed for speed, not stability. Many people report getting violently ill, and on one occasion, I saw reports of a broken bone.
While sea conditions vary from smooth as glass to rough chop in Galapagos, they tend to be more on the choppy side during the garua season (June - November in particular).
Such boats are maladapted to Galapagos sea condtions. In the days when I lived in the islands (before the on-set such high land-based tourism numbers), the interisland ferries were larger, more comfortable but slower ships - they could only do a one way trip / day, while today's speedboats do a return-trip every day. Perhaps it's time to reconsider the inter-island ferry model.

Galapagos: By Ship or by Land?
This is a very common question. Until the late 1990's, about 95% of visitors (75,000 people) to Galapagos embarked on a small (10-20 passenger) expedition (ta && ta.queueForLoad ? ta.queueForLoad : function(f, g){document.addEventListener('DOMContentLoaded', f);})(function(){ta.trackEventOnPage('postLinkInline', 'impression', 'postLinks-113451500', '');}, 'log_autolink_impression');cruise ship. In 2019, that number hadn't changed. But land-based visitor numbers had surged to 200,000. Why?
1) Internet: Thanks to Reddit, TripAdvisor, Blogs, websites etc... information on how to visit Galapagos became easy to find.
2) Price: A land-based visit can cost as little as $75 / day (if you're into rice, beans and a small piece of chicken, very basic accommodations and hanging out at the local beach).
3) The capacity of Galapagos fleet of cruise ships is capped - it is not allowed to grow, while there is no limit to how many land-based visitors can travel to the islands.
Pros of Land-Based
Cheaper. But if you do want to "get out among the islands" and experience what Galapagos has to offer, be prepared to budget from at least US$250 per person, per day. This will get you a day trip somewhere, a modest hotel and simple restaurants. Some land-based options are much pricier ($1,000 / day) - but you get what you pay for in terms of service, food, accommodations and day trips.
Evenings in town: If you're into socializing with the locals / other tourists, if you like the town scene / coffee shops etc... you might prefer a land-based trip.
Cons of Land-Based
You get less "Galapagos" for your time. Land-based visitors spend a lot of time each morning / afternoon "commuting" to and from visitor sites - sometimes up to three hours, depending on your destination.
Arriving at visitor sites when the sun is highest / hottest, and when wildlife tends to be resting / not as active / hiding in the shade.
Commuting to visitor sites / different islands on uncomfortable speedboats, where the likelihood of getting seasick is highest.
Evenings in town, where it can be noisy, full of lights.
Pros of Ship-Based
Your days are full. Not having to spend hours commuting, you are exposed to a lot more "Galapagos", with more time out and about, on land, under and on the sea.
You are visiting the more remote, less frequented parts of the islands, and doing so before / after the hottest part of the day, enjoying wildlife when it's most active (and when the lighting is best for photography)
You have the headspace to get into the "mind-trip" that comes with being far out at sea on a small ship, far removed from the noise and lights of town, under brilliant starry skies (an underrated part of the experience)
Cons of Ship-Based
Pricier: Expect to pay at least US$450 / person / day for a basic, but well-operated ship. You can pay as much as $1,250 or even more if you like. For those in the last minute market, up to 50% can be saved.
Sleeping / being on a ship: While there are never any storms in Galapagos, the seas can be choppy at times, making for some movement on board.
Fear of being with the same small group of people for too long.
CONCLUSION
If Galapagos for you is a vacation destination, where you're keen on mixing a wildlife adventure with socializing in town, checking out coffee shops, restaurants and meeting folks, you might prefer a land-based visit.
If Galapagos has been a long-time dream of yours, if you're very keen on getting exposed to as much wildlife and scenery as possible, then a ship-based visit might be more your style.
If a ship-based visit is what you'd like, but your budget doesn't allow it... wait. Save up. There's no rush.

TEXT:
The US National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration has published an advisory on the return of La Niña conditions in the tropical Pacific, with an 87% likelihood these conditions will last into the first months of 2022.
These would bring cooler and drier conditions to Galapagos. While usually warming up by January, waters would remain cool into at least February and maybe beyond, while the cool waters that usually are present until about December are even cooler now. This means that the use of a wetsuit - typically standard during the cool season (Jun to December) will likely also be appreciated these coming "warm" season months.
We would also expect less rainfall. On a typical year, most of the annual rainfall in Galapagos occurs between January and April, through occasional short-lived, but intense (and very impressive) tropical downpours. Less rain will result in less vegetation, fewer insects and a generally impoverished food chain for species that depend on terrestrial ecosystems. For instance, land birds (think Galapagos finches) may have more difficulties raising their young.

In its December 3rd press release #41, (see: https://www.turismo.gob.ec/udate-of-the-entry-requirements-to-ecuador/) the Ministry of Tourism indicated that the Ministry of Public Health had emitted new entry requirements. In this document, the following statement appears:

"The medical certificate applies to those entering Ecuador and the Galapagos."
