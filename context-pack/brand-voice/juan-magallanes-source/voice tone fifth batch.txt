Regulars on this forum know that I am partial to a ship-based visit (full disclosure, I run a small travel company and though we have helped people arrange land based trips, cruises are our specialty). Galapagos is an archipelago with widely dispersed islands. On a ship, you travel at night and you'll wake up each morning at your visitor site. You'll have engaged in activities such as snorkeling, hiking, kayaking and even paddle boarding (not all ships carry these - choose wisely) usually before any land-based visitor will have arrived at their first visitor site of the day (often after a 1-2 hour bone jarring speedboat ride). A Galapagos expedition cruise will be more active than an Antarctic cruise, as you'll be disembarking on average twice a day at 2 different visitor sites for slow hikes (slow, because it's about wildlife / nature observation, not about physical exertion, and that's done slowly), along with usually one snorkel, swim, kayak or other activity. As for timing, the Jan - April period is the hot season. Things will be hot, particularly between 10AM and 3PM. Waters will be warmer (a wetsuit is usually appreciated outside of the hot season) and generally calmer. You may experience an occasional spectacular tropical downpour, but they are short lived. Evenings will be comfortably warm. Vegetation will be lush (as lush as it gets in Galapagos). Wildlife is varied and fascinating any time of year in Galapagos.

In these uncertain COVID times, we are understandably concerned about the solvency of the travel agencies we book with, and the end suppliers you are reserved to travel with. While travel insurance will cover many things, another consideration is the regulatory framework in the jurisdiction under which the travel agency you book through operates.
In the United Kingdom, and in a few Canadian provinces (British Columbia, Québec, Ontario) and in some US states (California being one, there are others), travel agencies are strictly regulated. Some of these jurisdictions also have set up traveller compensation funds. If you book through an agency in such jurisdictions, even if you don't live there, you may be eligible for refunds from that fund if your agency or end supplier goes bankrupt. It's a good idea to ask your agent how the regulatory framework under which they operate protects your investment.

Dear BG,
1) Avoid travelling during school holiday periods to reduce the chances of having kids on board
2) Avoid big brand name ships that tend to the be the "go to" choice for those with kids (National Geographic, Celebrity...)
3) Avoid large (90 - 100 passenger) ships that tend to have more families on board.
4) Avoid "family departures" as advertised by some ships.
Having kids on board can actually contribute to a "more fun" trip, because kids are so enthusiastic, they help adults relax and join in on the fun. They can also incite the best from your naturalist guides. But of course, kids need to be well-behaved. If parents let their kids run around squealing and climbing on everything, it can certainly distract one's contemplative moments. If kids don't have the discipline to stay on the trails and the patience to spend lengths of time observing / contemplating nature around them, they could get fidgety / whiny, making things difficult for the rest of the group. These trips are expensive and you do want to have the option of avoiding being surrounded by "over-boisterous" kids.
There are about 65 ships plying Galapagos waters. Most carry 14-20 people (only about 6-7 carry 48 or more passengers). These ships offer a wide variety of comfort levels, from luxury / high end, to "no bells nor whistles". So, look around and let the ship representative / your travel agent know you want to be reassured on this matter.
My husband has done a careful analysis of ship itineraries. As you indicated, it's important to consider the relative merits of a ship's itinerary before committing. While you may think your itinerary was great, it may actually be a poor one. It's hard to compare itineraries if you do only one.
To determined the quality of an itinerary, has come up with the following indicators:
- Average # visitors sites / day (more is better),
- Distance sailed / visitor site (shorter is better - spending less time underway and more time engaged in various activities / anchored quietly)
- # of human settlements visited (fewer is better on the assumption that you'd rather be swimming with sea lions than shopping for t-shirts).
- # trip interruption (fewer is better - an interruption happens when a longer itinerary is made up of 2 shorter ones, where some passengers disembark / embark, and you stay on board)
The National Geographic itineraries are at best average using these criteria (the largest ships in Galapagos: 48 -100 passengers, all have average at best itineraries).

Dear MRC,
if you've found the ship you want to be on, and if that ship has a professional sales office that can help you will all your travel needs, there's no harm in working directly with it. If you're looking for the ideal ship / itinerary, working through a TA (assuming the TA is a Galapagos specialist) is a good way to proceed. A TA should not be charging you more (or less) than what the ship would charge if you book directly with the ship. Ship's pay agents via commissions, and if the ship makes the direct sale, they keep the commission. Of course, if you decide to go the agent route to find the ideal ship / itinerary, it's fair to conclude the deal with the agent.
Not all ships (especially the lower to mid-range ships) have a dedicated retail sales team. Most will rely on TAs to do all the marketing / outreach.
As for guides, there is no guarantee. There are a lot of qualities that go into being a good guide - and not all of these can be learned. Of course, higher end ships tend to pay more, and attract the better guides - the chances of getting at least a professional guide who knows his/her stuff and speaks a flawless English goes up with the price you pay, but even at that level, you'll get a lot of variation. It also depends on what you want in a guide. Some prefer a personable, enthusiastic guide over one that is more academic, for example, and vice versa.

Dear CB:
Every ship follows a different itinerary, and there are 65 ships. There is not single "northern loop" or "southern loop" itinerary. Some itineraries are as short at 4 days, or as long as 15 days.
The standard itinerary is 8 days / 7 nights. Assuming you're going to Galapagos with a singular focus on getting the most of what "Galapagos" is all about, then when selecting a ship, you should pay attention to its itinerary. A good 8 day itinerary will:
1) Be "uninterrupted". That means that it is not made up of two short 4 day itineraries. When that happens, you will need to get to a town / airport in the middle of your 8 day itinerary to pick-up and drop off passengers on the shorter itinerary. You may be wasting precious time during these transitions, and you might feel that the "group dynamic" with other passengers is negatively affected. Be sure your itinerary is uninterrupted.
2) Spends little time in human habitation zones. Assuming you're not in Galapagos to check out the cafes and t-shirt shops, make sure your itinerary doesn't have more than 1 stop in a human settlement (Puerto Ayora, (ta && ta.queueForLoad ? ta.queueForLoad : function(f, g){document.addEventListener('DOMContentLoaded', f);})(function(){ta.trackEventOnPage('postLinkInline', 'impression', 'postLinks-110063554', '');}, 'log_autolink_impression');San Cristobal Villamil, Floreana). Some ship itineraries will have you visit 2 or more towns... Not much fun when you'd rather be swimming with penguins, or coming across giant tortoises in the wild...
3) Maximum amount of visitor sites: Some itineraries include "Daphne Circumnavigation" (just motoring around an island - not very exciting!) or "whale watching" (meaning you are spending all afternoon motoring because the distance between 2 visitor sites is so lng) and not disembarking to explore and/or snorkel). Some 8 day itineraries have NOTHING planned on day 1 or day 8, as you arrive from the mainland, or disembark to catch your flight back. This is especially true for the larges ships (Celbrity, Lindblad, Silversea). Most smaller ships will have you visiting on day 1, and will also squeeze in a visit before going to the airport on day 8.
4) Logical path: Some itinerary maps look like spaghetti. The ship goes back and forth, all over the place. Other itineraries are very logical, never backtracking - these are better itineraries - as you spend less time motoring around the archipelago, and more time anchored quietly, and less pressure to get going becaus so many kms have to be covered.
For the reasons above, it's not necessary this loop or that loop that makes for a better itinerary. Any "loop" will give you a fantastic exposure to what Galapagos is about - and you'll get your money's worth much more if you consider the 4 points above.

Dear AP88,
things to consider when looking at itineraries (and I'm assuming you want to get the absolute most out of what Galapagos is famous for, and less interested in shopping / vsiting towns):
1) Minimum 7 nights / 8 days: Any shorter will leave you thinking you should have chosen a longer itinerary.
2) Uninterrupted: Be sure your 7 night / 8 day itinerary is not made up of 2 shorter segments, whereby some passengers on shorter itineraries are dropped off / picked up at the airport / town in the middle of your trip. Having to do that keeps the ship from heading off to the more remote parts of the archipelago, and sticking close to towns. Also, it usually disrupts the ambiance that develops between fellow passengers.
3) Try to find an itinerary that follows a logical route - and doesn't do all kinds of back-and-forths, following unecessarily circuitous routes. Such itineraries end up having you spend more time underway, with the motors running and possibly dealing with choppy seas. They may also be constrained by schedules and have little flexibility to veer off course from time to time to see whales, or to simply stop the engines and float near feeding orcas, or such things.
Most logically planned, uninterrupted 7night / 8 day itineraries will give you an excellent exposure to the best of what Galapagos has to offer.

That is a very short time. As noted, I spent 4 years there and didn't get enough... ;-)
The fisherman's market may be less active during COVID times, and it usually pretty much shuts down by mid-day (in normal times). The pier - well, it's a pier, with boats and you'll likely spot some sea lions or maybe rays swimming in the water. You may want to drop by after dark - sometimes you can spot different things in the shallow waters at night by the light of the street / pier lamps. It
s a good place for some air and a sense of activity / people watching. The trail to Tortuga Bay opens at 6AM - and it's about a 2 mile / 3km walk to the beach (the sun rises at about 6AM and will have move up quite a bit by the time you get there) - it's a nice walk that early as you are more likely to be alone. A very zen thing to do.
Is Black Turtle Cove accessible to day trippers? I'm not sure of that. Rules have been changing. But that's a multi-hour affair - as you need to get on a boat and motor over to the place. It might be hard to go to Tortuga Bay (for a swim also, I presume?), Black Turtle Cove (assuming it's open to day trippers) and then to Las Grietas in a day. North Seymour should have you run into blue footed boobies if they nesting (they nest opportunistically - and not according to a partcilar season). That will be a full day with good snorkeling. It's rated as a better than average visitor site.
These days new rules require you to hire a guide for Tortuga Bay, Grietas, Darwin Station's Giant Tortoise pens etc...
There are dozens of local agencies vying for your custom. But you might want to book your N. Seymour trip ahead of time, just to be sure and also to avoid having to spend any precious time in Galapagos shopping for tours instead of enjoying the place.

𝗪𝗵𝗮𝘁 𝗵𝗮𝗽𝗽𝗲𝗻𝘀 𝗶𝗳 𝘀𝗼𝗺𝗲𝗼𝗻𝗲 𝘀𝗵𝗼𝘄𝘀 𝘀𝘆𝗺𝗽𝘁𝗼𝗺𝘀 𝗼𝗳 𝗖𝗢𝗩𝗜𝗗 𝗼𝗻 𝗺𝘆 𝘀𝗵𝗶𝗽?
In July 2020, the Government of Ecuador established a clear protocol on how to handle suspected cases of COVID-19 on ships (crew or guests).
In the case of a suspected case of Covid-19 on a (ta && ta.queueForLoad ? ta.queueForLoad : function(f, g){document.addEventListener('DOMContentLoaded', f);})(function(){ta.trackEventOnPage('postLinkInline', 'impression', 'postLinks-109166579', '');}, 'log_autolink_impression');cruise ship, as soon as one passenger or crew member reports a symptom of Covid-19, the captain or first officer must report the suspected case to the Ministry of Health; the passenger or crew member in question must be isolated and centralized ventilation on the ship must be turned off.
The patient must then be interviewed by a doctor. The doctor may come from the ship of a suspected occurrence of Covid-19, a nearby ship, or a town (Floreana, Southern Isabela, Santa Cruz, or San Cristobal Island all have a doctor). The doctor must determine whether the symptoms presented are related to Covid-19, or another source, based on where the passenger has been, with whom they have gathered, as well as the size of any gathering the passenger has participated in over the previous 48 hours.
NB. In the event that no doctor is available to evaluate the suspected case, the patient must be isolated in his/her cabin and returned to port for a doctor’s evaluation. This may mean organizing a speedboat transfer of the person suspected of Covid-19 to the nearest health center for isolation and proper medical treatment, or detouring an entire cruise ship to port.
If the doctor determines that the symptoms in the suspected case did not originate from Covid-19, he/she will complete their report, leave the boat and it can continue with its itinerary.
If the doctor determines that the symptoms likely originated from Covid-19, the ship must return to port and the passenger must undergo an RT-PCR test. If the original suspected case is confirmed negative with a RT-PCR Test, the doctor will complete his/her report and the cruise ship can continue with its itinerary.
Should the suspected case yield a positive result to the RT-PCR test, the Ecuadorian Ministry of Health will interview all passengers and crew members in closest contact with the contagious passenger during the previous 48 hours to determine each person’s risk level, how many have symptoms, and whether an antigen or RT-PCR test needs to be carried out.
The vessel land office staff must assist the authorities in coordinating isolation of the patient onboard, in a Ministry of Health designated isolation accommodation on land (these are currently available on Santa Cruz, San Cristobal and Isbaela Islands), or even air transfer of the patient to a medical center in Quito or Guayaquil, or to their place of origin. All costs associated with handling COVID-19 related issues are the responsibility of the person(s) involved. Any passenger suspected of being a carrier of the disease must comply with the provisions that are issued through the representatives of the boat, and the Ministry of Health. Should additional passengers test positive for Covid-19, they too must isolate onboard or in a Ministry of Health designated isolation accommodation on land.

41 of 73 ships are currently operating in Galapagos, and all are following this Covid-19 protocol.
While these protocols are designed to reduce the spread of COVID-19, it is clear that they could severely disrupt a cruise. The requirement for a negative RT-PCR test prior to arriving in Galapagos goes a long way in reducing the chances of such an occurrence. Get vaccinated as soon as vaccines become available to you.
