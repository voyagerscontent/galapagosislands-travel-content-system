So that expectations are properly managed, there are no coral reefs per se in Galapagos. There is a bit of coral, but nothing like you might expect to see in the Caribbean or other famous coral places. The waters are too cool, generally, and the occasional 25 year El Niño warms up the waters to the point of baking the corals that survive in cooler waters. Underwater Galapagos is famous for its macrofauna - the big animals, like sea lions, penguins, sharks, rays, sea turtles, dolphins...

Black Turtle cove is a relatively small place where you can spot sea turtles, rays, sharks and birds from a zodiac / kayak. The park limits access to these places so as to avoid environmental degradation by over-use. Ship based visitation here has been pretty much constant for the past 15-20 years. But as land based tourism increases with no limits in sight (70,000 ship based visitors vs 180,000 land based visitors), pressure on the park service by land based tourism operators to "open up" sensitive areas to day trips increases. Indeed, if you're objective is to have extended hikes, you can do some in Galapagos, but you'll have to do most (all?) outside of the park boundaries.
Galapagos is world famous not for hiking, mountain biking / iron-man type of activities, but for the opportunity to get up close and personal with unique wildlife, to observe subtle differences between species first hand, so that you can better understand what biological evolution is all about and what a truly mind-expanding discovery it was for humanity (and of course, it's exhilarating to be with wildlife that is not afraid of you). Few if any other places on the planet can offer the visitor such graphic manifestations of how life evolves, and how it evolves within the context of a particular landscape. That's what got Darwin started on his revolutionary thinking back in 1835. The best way to connect with that fantastic discovery is to go slowly amid the park trails, the lava rocks and the beaches, the forests and to take the time to assimilate what you're seeing, and ideally, with some excellent guiding to help you along with your personal discovery.
Touting the hiking in Galapagos or the surfing, the night-life, the restaurants, or sports fishing (we're seeing more and more of all that) is missing the whole point - and frankly, demeaning to the iconic nature of the islands - it's like selling the Louvre in Paris by focusing on the food court there. If people are keen on extended and vigorous hikes, they'll find that opportunities are far far superior (and far far cheaper) on the mainland, with wildly diverse ecosystems, just about the richest bird life on the planet, plenty of quaint villages / towns, great places for a bite, snow capped volcanoes, rivers and a much more amenable climate (Galapagos is usually very hot for extended physical exertion e.g. collapsing in sweaty heaps happens to me after only 1 hour of steady hiking under the equatorial sun at sea level).
I'm unnerved that though more and more people are going to Galapagos, fewer and fewer seem to understand why the place is on the tourist map to start with. It is incumbent on the frequent posters here to occasionally remind folks on the "why" of Galapagos, and not to let them get distracted by bells and whistles and to help them be sure that their Galapagos holiday plans are motivated by the right reasons, and not just because it's a trendy thing to do / bragging rights back home.

Dear Romana,
I'm sorry you didn't enjoy Galapagos. I like to say that to get the most out of it, you have to prepare yourself not only for the physical trip (I bet you can't walk right up to nesting seabirds in Vancouver, nor to the iguanas in Mexico, or lie down next to sea lions in Vancouver and have them all look at you as if they are asking "what do YOU want?", nor swim with sea turtles, penguins and sea lions while actually having to make an effort not to touch them), but also for the mind trip.
If you can get into Darwin's head-space while there, you can enter a whole new and for some, much more profound dimension of the visit. After all, it's here that Darwin started cluing in on what was to become one of the most revolutionary soul-wrenching ideas in modern history. The marine iguana is an ugly sea going reptile - but a closer looks gives you a mind-altering understanding of biology, species, evolution and where humans fit on the planet. It's precisely because the iguanas in Galapagos resemble those on mainland Ecuador, but are different - that led to Darwin starting to ask questions.
Also, it's important not to consider visiting Galapagos a just an opportunity to tick off a "seen that animal" check-list. You can do that at any good zoo. It's about understanding what the hoopla is all about.
I can't help but to include a Darwin quote - the one that gives me goosebumps every time I read it:
"Seeing every height crowned with its crater, and the boundaries of most of the lava-streams still distinct, we are led to believe that within a period, geologically recent, the unbroken ocean was here spread out. Hence, both in space and time, we seem to be brought somewhat near to that great fact — that mystery of mysteries — the first appearance of new beings on this earth."
Another thing I like to say is that you have to manage your expectations - at the end of the day, you are in a very remote, very sparsely populated part of the world, in a developing country and on a wilderness / nature expedition. You have to be prepared to step "out of the bubble" when you go to Galapagos, with all its pros and cons.

Dear Aussie,
as we see here, tipping is rooted in cultural norms. In some countries where service industry folks are not paid so well, or whose jobs are not secure or come with few benefits, tipping is an important component of a pay package (in many cases too, it ends up being tax free - when tipping is rolled into the price of your trip, then the staff's tips are necessarily declared).
In the USA, tipping is considered generous. USA visitors are the single largest group in Galapagos - hence the service industry in Galapagos has come to expect that type of tipping.
Crew members on a ship typically make in the US$1,000/month range and benefit from basic social security coverage. It's a decently paid job in Galapagos - but not an easy one. After all, they live away from home for a good part of the year, sleeping mostly in cramped, often windowless berths shared with other crew. It's a tough job and if they do it well, it's because they are real professionals. Guides are among the best paid people in Galapagos, particularly for good ones. But they also have it hard, living away from families and doing the same thing over and over and over again. If your guide does it as if it's the first time and appears as enthusiastic and thrilled as you are, you know he/she's top quality.
I like to suggest that you tip a percentage of the price you paid for the "(ta && ta.queueForLoad ? ta.queueForLoad : function(f, g){document.addEventListener('DOMContentLoaded', f);})(function(){ta.trackEventOnPage('postLinkInline', 'impression', 'postLinks-91337324', '');}, 'log_autolink_impression');cruise only" component of your trip (leave out the flights, park fee, hotels etc...), and tip in the 2.5% range for that for i) the guide and 5% for ii) the whole of the rest of the crew. An 8 day, $4,000 cruise comes to $100 for the guide and $200 for the rest of the crew = $300 per person. This is 7.5% of the total price of the cruise. $300 / person is a relatively small amount compared to the overall price of your holiday (with all the other costs, likely near the $6,000 / person range - so, $300/$6,000 x 100 = 5%). Don't forget, the $200 for the crew is divided by approximately 8 people on a 16 passenger ship - you're tipping them $12.50 each).

Shark bites are extremely rare in Galapagos. The website sharkattackdata.com (honestly, you can find anything on the internet) reports 7 such attacks since 1954 (the site mistakenly categorizes an 8th attack in Santa Elena as a Galapagos attack). Here they are, summarized:
1954: Fisherman standing on a submerged platform, bitten on the foot
1959: Tuna fisherman swept overboard into a school of fish, bitten in the leg and foot
2007: Surfer, bitten in the thigh, San Cristobal island
2008: Tourist, bitten in the leg, Santa Cruz Island
2009: Surfer, bitten in leg, Isabela Island
2014: Surfer, Tortuga Bay, Santa Cruz Island, bitten in the calf
2015: Snorkeler, Punta Vicente Roca visitor site, bitten in the calf.
Most of them have occurred in the past 10 years - in line with the massive growth in land based tourism and thus the growth in the number of people in the water. However, given that there are way more people in the water engaged in snorkeling and swimming throughout the archipelago than there are surfers (who surf in just 3 or 4 places), it seems that surfing is way more risky than snorkeling / swimming. Perhaps the kind of sharks that are pre-disposed to bite tend to frequent surf type environments (where perhaps, in the low visibility turbulent waters it's easier to confuse a leg with a sea lion pup?).
Despite this recent incident, it's important to look at the numbers rationally. Over the past 10 years, approximately 1,400,000 people would have gone snorkeling or swimming in Galapagos waters, each person having jumped in on several occasions and in different parts of the archipelago. Only 3 (counting this latest incident) visitors not engaged in surfing have been bitten. The chances of getting bitten by a shark in Galapagos are microscopic. I would be more worried about a car accident on the way from or to the airport in Quito.
