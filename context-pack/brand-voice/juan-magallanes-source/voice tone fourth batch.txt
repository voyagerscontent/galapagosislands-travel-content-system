Dear DF,
indeed, there are many resources available on-line designed to help you plan your trip. The main issue, usually, is (ta && ta.queueForLoad ? ta.queueForLoad : function(f, g){document.addEventListener('DOMContentLoaded', f);})(function(){ta.trackEventOnPage('postLinkInline', 'impression', 'postLinks-103922355', '');}, 'log_autolink_impression');cruise vs land-based visit. Cruises are usually more expensive (e.g. be willing to budget at least US$450/person/day - and up to $1,200 if money is not object), but they offer the best way to max out on the best of what Galapagos has to offer, taking you around this collection of dispersed islands in the most convenient way, waking up at new, remote visitor sites, and spending more time enjoying the diverse ecosystems and wildlife, both above and below the water.
If you're constrained by budget, you can either i) wait and save up or ii) take a land-based trip. In this case, you stay in hotels and every day, you engage in different activities - but since you have to get from your bed to distant visitor sites, and back, every day, you invariably spend a lot of time in transit. You are also limited to visitor sites located nearer to the main population centers.
Before you buy an international flight, be sure you've decided on your course of action. If you're planning on a cruise, it is critical that you nail down the cruise before you organize your international flights - if you do it the other way around, you may find yourself wedged into a narrow travel window within which there will be limited cruise options.
Before you go, be sure of your motivations. It's an expensive destination, and excessive tourism eventually has some negative impacts on the destination.
Galapagos is famous and worth visiting for these reasons: The diversity of unique ecosystems and the animal and plant species that live there, how in most cases, the animals have no fear of humans and can be easily approached and finally, perhaps most significantly, to try to understand how a place could have such an important role in helping Charles Darwin's revolutionary thinking on how new species came about on the planet, in a time where the popular thinking was that all life had been created by god - end of story.
Galapagos is not about surfing, the bar scene, mountain biking, beaches, restaurants, iron-man wilderness treks, culture etc. etc... if any of that is what mostly appeals to you, you'll find much much better places to experience them elsewhere. Sadly, that side of Galapagos tourism has grown a lot in the past several years, catering to an increasing number of visitors who seem to be mostly focused on hanging out in a funky place, rather than in immersing themselves in those aspects that make Galapagos truly unique on a planetary scale.

Dear Tourist,
I once consulted marine architects on the question of catamaran vs monohull (of the same relative size). Their answer: It's not the chop that causes seasickness, but the uuuuuupppp and dooooowwwwwnnnn motion caused by the longer period swells. In that regard, both ships would perform identically.
As for the chop, each ship will experience it differently, depending on if it's heading into the chop, perpendicular to it, or on an angle. In short, they said "No real difference!".
As for larger (100 passenger) vs small (20 or fewer) - I'm partial to the smaller ships. You're visiting a remote archipelago and your objective is to get up close and personal with the wildlife, to feel like your sneaking a look at ecosystems / not intruding. Anchoring off a remote shoreline with just a handful of fellow passengers is more magical in my mind - it creates a greater sense of intimacy with your surroundings. You feel more like a special guest rather than an intruder.
Having said that, the 100 passenger ships won't feel the chop as much and there's more space on board of course.

Dear Colombia,
the unscientific popularization of Darwin's work is what links his time in Galapagos with his "Eureka I found it!" moment. In reality, he had already been wondering about how "new life" arrives on earth for a bit, but it took him another 24 years following his round the world trip (which brought him to Galapagos in 1835) - before he had the courage to publish his work, and the only reason he published "so soon" was because another person was independently arriving at the same conclusions, and was about to take the glory (Alfred Russell Wallace).
Darwin arrived in Galapagos having seen sea shell fossils up high in the Andean mountains .. "hmmmm... I guess the earth's crust moves around a lot - it goes up and down.... these rocks were once under the ocean... and not they are way up high here...hmmm....".
In Galapagos - he saw very fresh lava fields and even islands that were amost only just lava.... "hmmmm ... these islands must have emerged out of the sea in recent geological time.... not too long ago, there was no island at all here.... hmmmm....". On those islands, he saw a type of iguana that was completely new to science, but looked a lot like the ones he saw on the mainland.... "hmmm.... funny how there is this very complex creature here - a marine iguana - but how did it get here if the island it lives on just came into existence not too long ago? And isn't if funny that it looks a LOT like the iguana I saw on the Ecuadorian mainland a few weeks ago - but it's different... it's like they were related.... hmmmm....".
With that kind of observation, repeated many times for different species in different places, he was eventually compelled, despite the very strong religious beliefs he had (he was supposed to become a priest/pastor in the church before going his Beagle voyage), he just HAD to conclude that life did change over time - that species evolved, that they had not all just been created by god one day, and put there, as was the popular belief in those days.
Amazing. Galapagos is a good place to get close to Darwin's "head space" if you can take the time, look around and try to see what he saw.
I would also add that having the luxury of being able to drive a car 3,000 km through Ecuador for fun is something many people in Medellin would consider only possible for a rich person too. It's all a matter of your relative wealth (in money and in time).

Finally, trails can be steep, stair-ed, rocky, and often uneven, and so solid gross motor skills really are needed to prevent injury (even with walking sticks).
While many ship owners will make accommodations for less physically able guests (such as providing a panga ride rather than an arduous hike or extended snorkel) you must know before booking that if your guide feels that you are not capable of participating in an activity safely, they are obligated to ask you to sit the activity out.
On any Galapagos cruise, your naturalist guide will take his/her cue from the slowest member of the group (which under normal circumstances is not a terrible thing since it is assumed that you wish to take your time and really immerse yourself in the wildlife experience).
If you feel that a cruise may not best meet your needs, there an Ecuadorian company, Ecuador for All, that specializes in adapted trips that don't involve a cruise but do recognize the special needs of the less mobile.

booking in advance vs last minute bookings means you will likely be paying the standard price.
By paying the standard price in advance, you get more for your money. You get: i) choice of a wider range of availabilities / ships / trips, and not only the crumbs that are left over; ii) flexibility on exact date of the service you're booking, not being forced to settle on less than ideal travel dates; and iii) peace of mind - knowing that your travel plans are all organized and you won't have to be worrying at night, wondering if you'll be able to book the trip you want or not, for weeks and weeks prior to your trip. It boils down to how risk averse you are. The more in advance you book a ship, the more options you'll have to consider in terms of ship quality, reputation, itinerary, cabin. You'll have more time to check on the reputation of a ship before being pressed to book (highly recommended).

Dear CT,
as Brabb notes, it`s more complicated that that (things usually are more complicated than they appear).
The Galapagos islands emerged from the sea 1,000km away from the nearest land, as underwater volcanoes slowly built up. They were sterile rocks. Over the years, plants and animals from the mainland have been able to cross the 1,000km of open ocean to establish themselves there. All the native plants and animals you see in Galapagos somehow made it across 1,000km of ocean.
Besides insects, many of which can be airborne for a long time, or can get caught up in the feathers of birds, the only vertebrates who made it across in any number were the reptiles. Tortoises, iguanas, snakes, lizards.. because they can survive a long time without fresh water and food. That`s why Galapagos ecosystems are closer to what ecosystems were on Earth during the age of the dinosaurs...
Mammals die soon after being cut off from water and food and very few made it across 1,000 km of ocean in 5 million years since the first Galapagos land emerged from the sea. The are only 2 native mammal species in Galapagos: 1)rats - they are the toughest of all mammals. But they arrived a long time ago and evolved into new species (Galapagos rice rat) - today they are nearly extinct, mostly because when humans arrived, they brought more mammals, such as the norway rat, cats etc... that made life very difficult for the rice rat.
2) The only other mammal that made it across were bats, who managed to fly over.
So, getting rid of the very recent intruder will help the native rats and also birds whose eggs/nestlings are eaten by norway rats.

Hotel-Based Tours vs. Expeditions by Cruise: Choosing Your Galapagos Adventure
Having lived in Puerto Ayora for four years, worked at the Charles Darwin Research Station, and raised my children in the islands, I constantly get asked the ultimate question: should you visit the Galapagos Islands by taking an expedition cruise, or by doing a hotel-based (land-based) island-hopping tour
? The debate is never-ending, but understanding the stark differences will help you make the best choice for your bucket-list trip
.
If you are traveling to the Galapagos, your primary motivation is likely nature observation—getting up close and personal with unique wildlife and otherworldly landscapes
. Here is a breakdown of how the two travel styles compare in delivering that experience.
Expeditions by Cruise (Ship-Based Tours) A Galapagos cruise is not a typical mega-ship vacation with casinos and endless buffets; it is a true "ocean safari" designed to maximize your exposure to the archipelago's diverse ecosystems
.
The Pros:
Efficient use of your limited time: You travel at night, meaning you wake up already anchored at your morning visitor site
.
Optimal wildlife viewing: You will be out hiking and snorkeling early in the morning and late in the afternoon when the wildlife is most active
.
Access to remote corners: Cruises take you to distant, uninhabited islands (like Fernandina and Genovesa) that day-trippers simply cannot reach, giving you a profound sense of isolation
.
Seamless travel logistics: Once you board the ship and unpack in your cabin, you never have to worry about finding restaurants, packing your bags, checking in and out of hotels, or waiting for taxis
.
Intimate encounters: Most expedition ships carry 16 to 20 passengers, ensuring a highly personalized experience with your certified naturalist guides
.
The Cons:
Higher upfront costs: Cruises typically cost between 50% to 100% more than a land-based trip of a similar comfort level, with standard prices ranging from $350 to over $1,100 per person, per day
.
Digital detox: Internet and cell service are scarce, usually only available when the ship is anchored near main towns
.
Hotel-Based Tours (Land-Based / Island Hopping) A land-based tour involves staying in towns like Puerto Ayora (Santa Cruz) or Puerto Villamil (Isabela) and taking daily speedboat trips to nearby visitor sites
.
The Pros:
Budget-friendly flexibility: You have complete control over your spending
. You can survive on as little as $60 a day at modest hostels and local eateries, or spend $800+ a day at high-end boutique hotels
.
Free time and local culture: You have the freedom to skip a day of touring to just relax at a beach, enjoy the local town culture, and try different restaurants at your own pace
.
The Cons:
Exhausting daily logistics: A significant portion of your daylight hours is consumed by "commuting"
. You will spend hours getting to and from the docks, packing and unpacking between islands, and waiting for group members
.
Bone-jarring speedboat rides: Traveling to visitor sites often requires enduring 2 to 3-hour rides on small, fast speedboats
. In my experience, these rough rides induce seasickness far more often than the slower, stable cruise ships
.
Poor timing for wildlife: National Park regulations prevent land-based day-trippers from arriving at shared visitor sites before 10:00 AM
. By this time, the equatorial sun is at its zenith, and the animals are hiding from the heat to rest
.
Restricted access and crowds: Due to the skyrocketing number of land-based tourists, the National Park has restricted day-trippers from certain iconic snorkel sites, like Kicker Rock, to manage the overwhelming pressure on areas close to inhabited islands
.
The Conservation Reality It is vital to recognize the environmental footprint of your choice. Cruise ship capacity has been strictly capped for over 20 years, remaining flat at around 70,000 to 72,000 passengers annually
. Meanwhile, land-based tourism has exploded from nearly zero in the 1990s to over 200,000 visitors a year, leading to overcrowding at local sites and increased pressure on town infrastructure
.
The Final Takeaway If your primary goal is to get the absolute most "Galapagos" out of your visit—meaning you want intimate, high-quality encounters with wildlife and want to witness the graphic manifestations of biological evolution—an expedition cruise is hands-down the superior choice
.
However, if your budget is strictly limited, or you highly value unstructured free time in town over maximizing your time in the wilderness, a land-based trip is a completely viable way to experience the Enchanted Isles
.

Discuss Snorkeling and wildlife.

Snorkeling in the Galapagos is arguably half of the "WOW" factor of the entire trip
. Unlike destinations such as the Caribbean, there are no coral reefs here
. The underwater experience is entirely focused on marine megafauna.
Wildlife Encounters You can expect intimate encounters with unique marine life. Common sightings include:
Sea lions, which often act like "underwater puppies" and frolic around you
.
Sea turtles in such abundance that it can feel like swimming in "turtle soup"
.
Galapagos penguins darting through the water like torpedoes
.
Schools of rays (such as spotted eagle rays or mobula rays) swimming in formation
.
Harmless sharks, including Galapagos sharks and hammerheads
.
Rare but possible encounters with dolphins, orcas, or whale sharks
.
While the animals generally do not fear humans, it is critical to remember they are wild. You must maintain a 2-meter distance at all times and NEVER attempt to touch or pet them, as sea lions can deliver a dangerous bite
.
Water Conditions and Gear Galapagos waters are nutrient-rich, meaning visibility is often murky and varies from 7 to 50 meters
. However, this is rarely a problem because the wildlife comes so close to you
.
Hot Season (January to April/May): Waters are warmer, typically between 70F and 77F (20C to 25C)
. A "shorty" wetsuit or simply a swimsuit is often sufficient, depending on your tolerance
.
Cool/Garua Season (June to December): Water temperatures drop significantly. A 3mm to 5mm full wetsuit, along with a neoprene beanie and booties, is highly recommended to prevent chilling
.
Sun Protection: The equatorial sun is brutal. Wearing a full-length Lycra dive skin or long-sleeved rash guard will protect your back and thighs from severe sunburn and occasional stinging organisms like thimble jellyfish
.
Skill Level You do not need to be a strong swimmer to enjoy snorkeling, as the activity simply requires floating on the surface with your face in the water
. If you are apprehensive, naturalist guides and panga (dinghy) drivers are always nearby to assist
. You can wear a snorkeling vest for extra buoyancy, but avoid traditional life jackets, which are designed to keep your head out of the water and make snorkeling difficult
.
If you choose to sit out the snorkeling excursions, you can often opt for a panga ride along the shoreline to spot wildlife, and a few larger ships even offer glass-bottom boats
.
